Course 1202: Elicitation - Elicitation Protocol
Collapse all

One of the most powerful analysis tools built into SALT is the ability to compare an individual language sample to age or grade-matched peers selected from the reference databases. In order for the comparison to be valid, however, the language samples should be elicited following the same protocol as was used to collect the database samples. In this course, you will learn about the SALT reference databases and how to collect comparable samples.

Materials Needed: Adobe Reader or other PDF reader which is needed to view document files.

In-person or Virtual Elicitation

The protocols described in this course cover both in-person and virtual sessions. Most language samples can be elicited in either type of session with similar results (Manning, et al., 2020, Taking Language Samples Home: Feasibility, Reliability, and Validity of Child Language Samples Conducted Remotely with Video Chat Versus In-Person).

Protocols Used with SALT Databases

Whether we elicit samples in person or virtually, we need to be consistent and make sure that our assessment objectives are met. There are any number of protocols you can follow to elicit language samples. When planning to compare your sample with age or grade-matched samples selected from one of the SALT reference databases, it is important that you follow the specific protocol for that sampling context. The following sections include information on the SALT databases available for comparison and provide examples of the elicitation protocol used to collect the samples.

Work through each of these sampling protocols, reading about the corresponding SALT databases and listening to sample elicitations.

Play Samples - Preschoolers

Play-based Conversational Samples

Conversational samples, even from young children, target pragmatic language skills including turn taking, topic maintenance, and responding to contingent speech. They provide opportunity to document social and parent-child interaction. Children between four and five are in a transitional period where some of these children may be able engage in a direct conversation, while others will still require the play setting to elicit a comfortable, reliable, and valid language sample. This section focuses on collecting conversational samples from preschoolers following the play-based protocol used to collect the samples contained in the SALT Play database. Conversation is what we do all the time. It's important to reflect on how we can record a reasonable and productive conversation.

READ the Play database elicitation protocol.

Tips for collecting a play-based sample

The database samples were elicited with an examiner engaged in play with the child and is the preferred method of elicitation. However, if the child is reticent to play with the clinician or if you are eliciting the sample in a virtual session, the parent/guardian can elicit the sample. The parent/guardian may need to be counseled, however, to let the child talk spontaneously and not just respond to a series of yes/no questions. 
Take the sample in a quiet environment. Set up the toys on a carpet or cloth to minimize the clanging and banging of objects.
Toys that can be manipulated work best, e.g., farm set, gas station, play-dough.
Samples should be 5-10 minutes in length.
Record sample for later transcription using your phone, tablet, or digital recorder. In a virtual session, you have the additional option of recording the sample using the recorder built into the software you are using to host the virtual meeting, e.g., Zoom.

Conversational Samples - Age 5 and Older

Conversational samples can be collected throughout the life span. They target pragmatic language skills including turn taking, topic maintenance, and responding to contingent speech in conversation. They provide opportunity to document social and classroom interaction. Conversational samples elicited for persons five years and older can be done by establishing a conversation between that person and an examiner. Develop a conversation using the following standard set of topics that have been used successfully over the past number of years and are the ones used to collect the samples contained in the SALT Conversation database:

Classroom activities, as many samples will likely be collected in a school setting.
Holidays, which provide an opportunity to talk about past and future events.
Family activities, which may also include holidays and family pets.



Time versus sample size

The amount of time it may take to collect a sample of sufficient size is, of course, an issue. The goal is to collect a sample between five and ten minutes that is representative of the speaker's general language performance. For most measures, a five minute sample is sufficient. A ten minute sample provides more opportunity for variation in vocabulary, syntactic structures, and semantics or meanings represented. For some speakers a ten minute sample is necessary where you are exploring in detail one or more of these features.

To explore a little more detail and provide some interesting data relative to what to expect, take a look at these two graphs which compare the number of words and utterances produced for typical children ages 5 - 13. The dark blue represents the five-minute samples and the light blue represents the 10-minute samples. When looking at the number of words produced in a five minute sample versus a ten minute sample (left graph), you can see the numbers are reasonably proportional. You should also note that the amount of language by age continues to increase almost linearly. This is important to keep in mind as it allows us to expect that the older the child is, the less time it should take elicit a sample that has reliable outcomes. If you look at the number of utterances (right graph) you'll see that it's an alternative production of the same information. As children get older they produce not only longer utterances, but more utterances per unit time. So whether you use words or utterances, both produce the same picture. As children get older they talk more and this allows us to structure our language sample to get the maximum bang for the amount of time we're spending collecting the sample.

Tips for collecting a conversational sample

Avoid personal topics - that is, avoid discussing topics that are personal to the speaker and his or her family in order to respect the speaker's and family's privacy.
Avoid introducing narratives into your conversations, things such as movie plots, TV show, books, or stories. For example, suppose you're talking with a student about what they did the past weekend and the student says, "We went to see Avatar at a 3D theater". Feel free to talk about who the speaker went to the movie with or generally about 3D effects in movies. But don't ask for details about the movie plot as this will likely turn your conversation into a narrative.
Use open-ended questions. Avoid questions that elicit one-word responses.
Request clarification where appropriate as this let's the speaker know that you are listening carefully to what they have to say.
Don't be afraid of pauses - be patient and wait. We say this repeatedly because in English discourse a pause of greater than 2 seconds is generally seen as an opportunity to change speakers or to change turns. In this case we want to make sure the target speaker has as much opportunity to talk as possible.
Record sample for later transcription using your phone, tablet, or digital recorder. In a virtual session, you have the additional option of recording the sample using the recorder built into the software you are using to host the virtual meeting, e.g., Zoom.

Narrative Story Retell Samples

The Story Retell protocol uses specific stories. Knowing the story target allows you to evaluate student performance, looking at what was left out as well as what was included, such as specific vocabulary and content circumlocutions. Individual performance is compared with database samples from typical students retelling the same story. This provides opportunity to compare overall story retell at the narrative structure level as well as the word, morpheme, and utterance level. This section focuses on collecting samples following the protocol used to collect samples contained in the SALT Narrative Story Retell database. Refer to the online course 1602 for the protocol used to collect samples contained in the SALT Bilingual Spanish/English and Monolingual Spanish databases.

READ the Narrative Story Retell database elicitation protocol.

Age Consideration

Our research suggests that this narrative task is not suitable for typical children younger than around 4- 5 years of age. This is to say that young children usually 1) don't have a good sense of story structure, and 2) even if they do, they're young enough in the language acquisition processes that they don't necessarily have the ability to retell the entire story. On the other end of the scale, the task, in its current form, may be too simplistic for students older than around age 13 - 14.

The Protocol

Story retell protocols begin with providing a model of the story while the student looks through the book. The student is then asked to retell the same story in his or her own words using the pictures in the book. If the book contains words, the words are covered for the retell.

Tips for collecting a narrative story retell sample

In an in-person session, move slightly away from the speaker, turning so that eye contact is easy. The speaker should be in charge of page turning during the retell but provide assistance if the speaker has trouble properly turning pages. Moving away from the speaker promotes language and minimizes pointing.
Use minimal prompts as necessary. If the speaker is unable to start, use the prompt "One day". If the speaker stops talking before the story is finished, say, "Tell me more", "Keep going", "You are doing a great job", "And then". You may also use nonverbal cues such as head nodding and smiling to promote continued talking. Using overly-specific questions or providing too much information compromises the process of capturing the speaker's true language and ability level. Open-ended prompts do not provide answers or vocabulary. They do encourage the speaker to try, or the speaker know it is ok to move on if needed. Avoid asking the "wh" questions, who?, what?, when?, where? as these often lead to obvious and limited responses/answers.
Don't be afraid of pauses - be patient and wait. We say this repeatedly because we want to make sure the target speaker has as much opportunity as possible to tell the story.
Record sample for later transcription. If you are using SALT's Online Story Elicitation program in an in-person session, use the recorder built into the program. Otherwise, use your phone, tablet, or digital recorder. In a virtual session, you have the additional option of recording the sample using the recorder built into the software you are using to host the virtual meeting, e.g., Zoom.

Narrative SSS (Speaker Selects Story) Samples

Narrative language is considered to be the most relevant for school performance. Oral narrative skills are associated with learning to read and are part of most state and school district standards. Narrative samples provide information about the speaker's ability to maintain story structure, relate the the order of events, and describe the relationship of story characters. SALT Software has several different narrative databases including: Speaker Selects the Story (SSS), Story Retell, Expository, and Persuasion. This section focuses on collecting samples following the protocol used to collect the samples contained in the SALT Narrative SSS database.

Age Consideration

Our research suggests that this narrative task is not suitable for typical children younger than around 4- 5 years of age. This is to say that young children usually 1) don't have a good sense of story structure, and 2) even if they do, they're young enough in the language acquisition processes that they don't necessarily have the ability to retell the entire story that they're attempting to relay to the examiner.

The Protocol

With this protocol, you ask the speaker to tell you about a favorite movie or book. We began this type of narrative in an effort to get speakers to be most familiar with their own story or movie; feeling that selecting their own movie would motivate them to talk more and we think this worked very well. One of the restrictions of this type of narrative, of course, is that the speakers talk about different kinds of stories, movies, TV programs; making it impossible to evaluate an individual student's vocabulary, for example, with the database in anything other than general terms. In any event it tells us something a great deal more than a conversational sample does, particularly in the amount of complex syntax. And in fact speakers are more likely to produce longer utterances in this type of narrative than they are in conversation.

Ask the speaker to identify a favorite movie, story, or book he/she would like tell you about. Movies and books are best; TV shows may have not have a sequential plot as they did in the old days when we first began this enterprise - where there were TV programs that had the same characters engaged in a new plot and activity each week. Prompt the speaker with, "Tell me about this movie or book. I'm not very familiar with it so you'll have to give me a lot of background information about the story so I can follow it. Start at the beginning and tell me the whole thing from the beginning, middle, to the end."

It's important to be patient and wait. Refrain from discussion which may lapse into a conversation. Don't be afraid of pauses. The more you wait the more the speaker will feel it's their turn to continue to talk. Encourage the speaker with open-ended, generic questions and comments such as, "What happens next?", "Keep on going", "You're doing a good job." Use head nods, eye contact, and smiles for encouragement. Ask for clarification when necessary but try to keep verbal input to a minimum. Listen, don't discuss, as in a conversation.

Expository Samples

Expository language samples are considered to be very relevant for secondary school performance and can be tied to state or district standards for oral language. For example, the Wisconsin standards for oral language states that by the end of grade 8, students will, speaking from notes or an outline, relate an experience in descriptive detail, with a sense of timing and decorum appropriate to the occasion. And by the end of grade 12, students will develop and deliver a speech that conveys information and ideas in logical fashion for a selected audience, using language that clarifies and reinforces meaning (Wisconsin Department of Public Instruction, 1998). Also see Using SALT to Assess the Common Core.

Exposition is both challenging and motivating for students. It is unbiased, reflecting the students' knowledge and interest across gender, age, and culture. It features standard rules and vocabulary that are easily accessible and provides a check on students' accuracy.

This section focuses on collecting samples following the protocol used to collect the samples contained in the SALT Expository database where the students explain a favorite game or sport using a planning sheet.

Age Consideration

Expository samples within the SALT reference database were collected from students beginning in 5th grade and are available for database comparison. It is our experience that typical children in younger grades lack the skills needed to use a planning sheet effectively in order to explain how to play a game or sport.

The Protocol

The elicitation protocol is easy to administer and provides optimum opportunity for the student to produce a "good" exposition. Following a script, the examiner asks the student to explain how to play a game or sport of the student's choosing. The student is given a few minutes to complete a planning sheet which contains eight components of an exposition (Object, Preparations, Start, Course of play, Rules, Scoring, Duration, and Strategies). Listed next to each component is a brief description of what's covered within that component and space for making notes. Following the planning phase, the student is asked to explain the game or sport using his/her notes. The average length of the exposition is between 5 - 6 minutes and between 50 - 60 complete and intelligible utterances.

Elicitation method

The sample may be elicited with the speaker seated near you or via a virtual session. Both methods should result in similar outcomes (Manning, et al., 2020, Brennan, et al., 2004). All samples should be recorded for later transcription.

In-person session: Seat the speaker near you, either across the table or next to you. Have the planning sheet ready to hand over. Record the sample using your phone, tablet, or digital recorder.
Virtual session: If feasible, send the planning sheet to the speaker with instructions to print it for the session. If this is not convenient then, when it comes time for planning, read each item on the planning sheet and give the speaker time to make notes. Record the sample using your phone, tablet, digital recorder, or the recorder built into the software you are using to host the virtual meeting, e.g., Zoom.

READ the Expository database elicitation protocol.

Tips for collecting an expository sample

Follow the elicitation procedure.
Give the student adequate time to use the planning sheet and allow them to refer to it during the exposition.
Avoid asking specific questions or engaging in conversation.
Don't be afraid of pauses - be patient and wait.
Record for later transcription. Include students' date of birth and date of elicitation on recording.

Does the type of sport or game selected affect the quality of the expository?

Our first set of expository samples came from 87 participants, ages 12;7 - 15;9 living in two urban areas in Wisconsin. To answer this question, we looked at the type of sport or game selected. 62% of the participants selected a team sport such as baseball or volleyball, 18% selected an individual sport such as tennis or swimming, and 20% selected a board game, card game, or other similar game. We looked at the effect of the type of sport or game selected on four language variables: mean length of utterance in morphemes (MLUm), number of different words (NDW), subordination index (SI), and expository scoring scheme (ESS). Separate ANOVA tests were completed, with the language variable as the dependent variable and the type of sport or game as the between groups variable. There were no significant differences.

IN SUMMARY

Exposition is central to the curriculum in middle and high school and is included as state standards for speaking and writing. It is unbiased and challenges students to use language in context.

Persuasion Samples

Persuasive samples are relevant for secondary school performance and can be tied to state or district standards for oral language. Persuasive skills in both oral language and written language are seen within the Common Core State Standards. By grade 12, students are expected to "Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11-12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively (Common Core Standard: Speaking and Listening 11-12.1). Also see Using SALT to Assess the Common Core.  

Persuasion is motivating for many secondary students as they have the opportunity to speak on a topic that is important to them and based on their own experiences. These samples are unbiased, as the student selects his or her own topic.

Age Consideration

Persuasive samples within the SALT reference database were collected from 9th-12th grade students and are available for database comparison. However, persuasive skills may be an area of interest beginning around 6th grade. 

The Protocol

The elicitation protocol is easy to administer and provides optimum opportunity for the student to produce a strong persuasive argument. Following a script, the examiner gives the student a list of suggested topics and asks the student to select the rule or situation he/she would like to see changed. The student can select a topic from the list or choose his/her own topic. Once the student selects a topic, the student is given a few minutes to complete a  planning sheet which contains six components of a persuasive argument (Issue Identification and Desired Change, Supporting Reasons, Counter Arguments, Response to Counter Arguments, Compromises, and Conclusion). Listed next to each component is a brief description of what's covered within that component and space for making notes. Following the planning phase, the student is asked to persuade someone with the power to make the change such as a principal or government official using his/her notes. The average length of the persuasion is between 3-5 minutes.

Elicitation method

The sample may be elicited with the speaker seated near you or via a virtual session. Both methods should result in similar outcomes (Manning, et al., 2020,  Brennan, et al., 2004). All samples should be recorded for later transcription.

In-person session: Seat the speaker near you, either across the table or next to you. Have the list of suggested topics and the planning sheet ready to hand over. Record the sample using your phone, tablet, or digital recorder.
Virtual session: If feasible, send the list of suggested topics and the planning sheet to the speaker with instructions to print them for the session. If this is not convenient, (a) when it comes time for choosing a topic, read the list to the speaker, and (b) when it comes time for planning, read each item on the planning sheet and give the speaker time to make notes. Record the sample using your phone, tablet, digital recorder, or the recorder built into the software you are using to host the virtual meeting, e.g., Zoom.
