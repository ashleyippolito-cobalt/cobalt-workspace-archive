# Section: Narrative SSS (Speaker Selects Story) Samples

<!-- https://classes.saltsoftware.com/course/section.php?id=40 -->

Section outline

Narrative language is considered to be the most relevant for school performance. Oral narrative skills are associated with learning to read and are part of most state and school district standards. Narrative samples provide information about the speaker's ability to maintain story structure, relate the the order of events, and describe the relationship of story characters. SALT Software has several different narrative databases including: Speaker Selects the Story (SSS), Story Retell, Expository, and Persuasion. This section focuses on collecting samples following the protocol used to collect the samples contained in the SALT Narrative SSS database.

Age Consideration

Our research suggests that this narrative task is not suitable for typical children younger than around 4- 5 years of age. This is to say that young children usually 1) don't have a good sense of story structure, and 2) even if they do, they're young enough in the language acquisition processes that they don't necessarily have the ability to retell the entire story that they're attempting to relay to the examiner.

The Protocol

With this protocol, you ask the speaker to tell you about a favorite movie or book. We began this type of narrative in an effort to get speakers to be most familiar with their own story or movie; feeling that selecting their own movie would motivate them to talk more and we think this worked very well. One of the restrictions of this type of narrative, of course, is that the speakers talk about different kinds of stories, movies, TV programs; making it impossible to evaluate an individual student's vocabulary, for example, with the database in anything other than general terms. In any event it tells us something a great deal more than a conversational sample does, particularly in the amount of complex syntax. And in fact speakers are more likely to produce longer utterances in this type of narrative than they are in conversation.

Ask the speaker to identify a favorite movie, story, or book he/she would like tell you about. Movies and books are best; TV shows may have not have a sequential plot as they did in the old days when we first began this enterprise - where there were TV programs that had the same characters engaged in a new plot and activity each week. Prompt the speaker with, "Tell me about this movie or book. I'm not very familiar with it so you'll have to give me a lot of background information about the story so I can follow it. Start at the beginning and tell me the whole thing from the beginning, middle, to the end."

It's important to be patient and wait. Refrain from discussion which may lapse into a conversation. Don't be afraid of pauses. The more you wait the more the speaker will feel it's their turn to continue to talk. Encourage the speaker with open-ended, generic questions and comments such as, "What happens next?", "Keep on going", "You're doing a good job." Use head nods, eye contact, and smiles for encouragement. Ask for clarification when necessary but try to keep verbal input to a minimum. Listen, don't discuss, as in a conversation.

IN SUMMARY

Student-selected stories optimize the students' motivation to talk because it's their story they want to convey to you. It's an opportunity to document narrative skill, the overall ability to organize a story beginning, middle, and end, so that is can be analyzed from a text level as well as a word and utterance level.
