Starting a Transcript

When you open the SALT program, you are presented with a dialogue box with the option to create a new transcript or open an existing transcript. 




If you are creating a new transcript you are presented with the "New Transcript-Header Information" dialogue box where you enter all pertinent information about your transcript such as the target speaker, other speakers, gender, date of birth, date of sample, sampling context and subgroup/story, target language, and initial clock time. There's also a section to add additional header information unique to your sample. Once you have filled in all required information a new transcript with a header is created.

After the transcript has been created with all the necessary header information, there are a few other critical formatting conventions for a SALT transcript. Beginning and ending clock times are marked with a hyphen (-). The speaker label line in the header identifies all speakers, therefore all speaker utterances need to start with the same letter as the speakers identified in the header, e.g., C for "Child" and E for "Examiner".  Between utterance pauses are marked with semicolon (;) if there is not a change in the speaker or a  colon (:) if there is a change in speakers. All speaker utterances end with standard punctuation such as a period, question mark, or exclamation point. Other ending punctuation includes tilde (~) for clinician prompts, carrot (^) for interrupted utterances, and a greater than sign (>) for abandoned utterances.

C-Unit Segmentation

Segmenting utterances into Communication Units (C-units) is the preferred choice for segmentation of language samples. C-units are syntax-based, which allows for greater consistency in utterance segmentation.  The SALT reference database transcripts are segmented using C-units, so if a database comparison is being completed, segmenting utterances into C-units is essential. 

C-Unit Definition:  An independent clause and all its modifiers, including subordinate clauses.

Examples

C I thought the frog jump/ed.	One C-unit with a main and subordinate clause.
C The rule/s are no hitting, no pushing and no face mask/s.	One C-unit with one main clause.
C And then the gnome said, "Becoming a flower is hard."	One C-unit with direct quote and two clauses.


C I went to my friend/z house this weekend.

C And we went to Big_Al's to bowl.

	Two C-units segmented by the coordinating conjunction "and."


C I/'m get/ing a new bike, which will be awesome.

C But I have to wait until my birthday.

	Two C-units. The first C-unit has a main and subordinate clause. The second C unit is segmented at the coordinating conjunction "but."


C You have to go fast so that you don't fall off your board.

	One C-unit with a main and subordinate clause.


C If we don't hurry we/'ll be late.

	One C-unit with a main and subordinate clause. Uses the conditional "if...then" structure with implied "then".

Tips

Look for conjunctions. Coordinating conjunctions, such as "and", "but", "so", and "then", usually signal a new C-unit. Subordinating conjunctions, such as "because", "that", "when", "while", "where", "unless", and "as__as", do not signal a new C-unit and should be included in the utterance.

Look for conditional structures. Clauses using conditional structures such as "if...then" and "either...or" are kept together in the same C-unit.

Segment Fragments.  Sentence fragments are counted as separate C-units if intonation indicates a complete thought has been spoken.

Elliptical Answers.  Elliptical responses, which are sentence fragments in response to a question, are counted as separate C-units. 

Sentence Tags.  When phrases such as "I guess", "you know", or "I mean", are used as tags to an utterance do not segment them into separate C-units. 

C-unit Segmentation Rules. Print this guide to refer to as you learn how to segment utterances using C-units. C-Unit Segmentation Rules

Bound Morphemes

Bound morphemes are an essential transcription convention. Grammatical morphemes are marked to measure the language development and complexity of young speakers. A specific set of inflectional morphemes are marked, but derivational morphemes are not marked (see this SALT blog for a detailed explanation). Bound morphemes are marked in SALT transcripts using a slash (/) to separate bound morphemes from free morphemes. Most of the bound morphemes are straight forward and easily recognized; however, several of them can be tricky when first learning to transcribe. See the tips below for more clarification. 

Bound Morphemes

/S

	

Plural

Examples: KITTEN/S, BABY/S

Do not mark words that end in "s" but represent one entity (e.g., PANTS, GLASSES). Do not mark irregular forms (e.g., MICE, GEESE, DEER) or when the sound of the root changes (e.g., LEAF --> LEAVES, WOLF --> WOLVES).




/Z

	

Possessive inflection

Examples: DAD/Z, MARY/Z.

Do not mark any possessive pronouns. Examples: MINE, HIS, HERS, OURS, YOURS, ITS, THEIRS




/S/Z

	

Plural and Possessive

Example: BABY/S/Z




/ED

	

Past tense

Examples: LOVE/ED, DIE/ED

Do not mark predicate adjectives (e.g., WAS TIRED, IS BORED).

Transcription hint: regular past tense /ED verbs will not follow a BE or GET verb.




/EN

	

Past participle

Examples: EAT/EN, TAKE/EN, PROVE/EN, 

Do not mark irregular forms (e.g., GOTTEN, SPOKEN, SEEN, BEEN) or when the sound of the root changes (e.g., WRITE --> WRITTEN). 

Transcription hint: Regular form: present tense + EN as separate syllable. /EN verbs always follow HAVE, HAS, or HAD.




/3S

	

3rd Person Singular verb form

Examples: GO/3S, TELL/3S, TRY/3S

Do not mark Irregular forms (e.g., HAS, WAS) or when the sound of the root changes (e.g., DO --> DOES, SAY --> SAYS).




/ING

	

Progressive verb form

Examples: GO/ING, RUN/ING, BIKE/ING

Do not mark the gerund use of the verb form (e.g., WENT SWIMMING, READING IS FUN).




/N'T, /'T

	

Negative contractions

Examples: CAN/'T, DOES/N'T

Do not mark irregular forms (e.g., WON'T) or when the sound of the root changes (e.g., DO --> DON'T). Do not mark the word AIN'T.




/'S, /'RE, /'M, /'LL, /'D, /'VE

	

Contracted --> IS, ARE, AM, WILL, WOULD, and HAVE

Examples: HE/'S, WE/'RE, I/'M, I/'LL, I/'D, WE/'VE




/H'S, /H'D, /D'S, /D'D, /'US

	

Contracted --> HAS, HAD, DOES, DID, and US.

Examples: HE/H'S been sick. WHAT/D'S he do for a living? Why/D'D the boy look for the frog? LET/'US go.

Tips

/ING versus Gerund

Recognizing gerunds can be confusing when transcribing. There is a tendency to mark all -ING endings, making them verb forms. However, many times words that end with -ing are not actually verbs; they are gerunds. Gerunds are verb forms that function as nouns, i.e., subjects, direct objects, indirect objects, and objects of prepositions, etc.

Gerunds

	

Verb Forms




C I went shopping.

C We went to the shopping mall.

C She start/ed bleeding.

C I/'m a singing machine.

	

C I am look/ing at it.

C I was try/ing.

C We will be go/ing.

C Just keep walk/ing.

/ED versus Predicate Adjective

Only mark the simple past tense verbs. Do not mark predicate adjective. Recognizing predicate adjectives can also be confusing when transcribing. A predicate adjective is an adjective that follows a linking verb and tells us something about the subject. If an ED verb follows a BE verb or a GET verb, it is a predicate adjective and should not be marked. 

Predicate Adjectives

	

Verb Forms




She/'s bored.

It get/3s congested.

He was tired.

	

He look/ed under the branch.

We play/ed outside.

It rain/ed yesterday.

Omissions, Errors, and Other Coding

Omissions and errors should be marked within a transcript to determine the potential of a language impairment based on language skills that are still developing, absent, or used incorrectly. There are several different conventions to mark omissions and errors.

Omissions

Both omitted words and omitted bound morphemes are marked with an asterisk (*). The asterisk is placed before the omitted word or before the omitted bound morpheme.

Examples

Omitted Words	

It *is here.

I want *to eat it.

The frog was not *in the jar.


Omitted Morphemes	

The car go/*3s fast.

I am make/*ing you a muffin.

He/*'s go/ing home.

Error Codes

SALT uses standard error codes in the database comparison samples. Error codes are marked using square brackets [  ]. Error codes can be used at the word level (attached to words) or at the utterance level (not attached to words). SALT database comparison transcripts use [EW:__] for word level errors, [EW] to mark extraneous words, and [EO:__] to mark overgeneralization errors. Root forms of words can also be coded when marking overgeneralization errors. [EU] is used to mark utterance-level errors. It is used when there are three or more errors in an utterance, or if there are syntax errors that are not easily repaired using word level codes. Note that the Setup menu --> Lists --> Standard Code Lists option is used to view or edit the list of codes which SALT recognizes as error codes.

Examples

Word-Level Errors	

Him[EW:he] is look/ing everywhere for his frog.

They is[EW:are] right there.

The more[EW] better to see you with my dear.

It was hises|his[EO:his] video game.

We wented|go[EO:went] to Grandma/z house.

There were peoples|people[EO:people] there.


Utterance-Level Errors	

I never not did that [EU].

The store go/ing to Grandma/z [EU].

And you/'re allowed to the first person with bat [EU].

Custom Codes 

Non-standard codes can also be used in SALT to mark features for which there isn't a transcript convention. Clinicians may be interested in examining pronoun use, appropriateness of responses to questions, dialect use, or disfluencies. Any code can be used as long as it it entered inside square brackets [  ], for example the code [IR] may be used to code an utterance that was an "inappropriate response" to a question, e.g., off topic.

Mazes and Part Words

Mazes are  filled pauses, repetitions, and revisions. Mazes can indicate word finding or utterance formulation difficulties. Some rules for marking mazes follow:

Enclose the maze in parentheses (  ).
Mark the earliest occurrence of the maze.
Combine sequential mazes.
Include pauses which occur immediately before, in the middle of, or immediately after, the mazed words.
End all part words with an asterisk (*).



For example, "We went to (um the :03) the store." Notice that the first production of "the" was mazed. The final word spoken is not marked as part of the maze. The filled pause and the repetition were included in the same maze and the pause immediately following the maze was also included.

Filled pauses: The default filled pause words include: AH, EH, ER, HM, HMM, MM, UH, and UM. Examples: "We went to the (um um) grocery store."  and "It/'s his (bir* er) birthday party."

Repetitions: To determine if a maze is a repetition SALT looks at what follows the maze. So it is important to mark the first occurring word(s) in the repetition as a maze. Examples: "She/'s (my my) my Aunt_Katie," and "(It/'s not um) it/'s not bad."

Revisions: If a maze component is not a filled pause or repetition then, by default, SALT considers it to be a revision. A speaker revises an utterance by choosing different words with the same semantic intent. Examples: "And then (I went) we went to the park." and "We (were) were/n't here."

Part words: Part words end with an asterisk (*) indicating that the rest of the word was omitted. Examples: "(We were go*) we went to the park." and "He (m* m*) made it."

Pauses

Marking pauses within a transcript can give clinicians valuable information about verbal rate and facility. Pauses can be telling of word finding, utterance formulation, or processing difficulties and are marked when they occur within an utterance or between an utterance. Pauses can be marked within and between utterances. All pauses of two seconds or longer were marked in the SALT database samples. Pauses do not have to be marked if this is not an area of interest. And, if marked, they do not have to be timed; they can be marked using just the semicolon or colon.

Pause times

If timed, mark the length of the pause in minutes:seconds or in :seconds. Examples:

Two-second pauses	

 :02

 0:02

 00:02


One-minute, two-second pause	

 01:02

 
Within-utterances pauses

Enter the pause where it occurs in the utterance. Separate the pause from the adjacent words with spaces. If the pause occurs immediately before or after a maze, include it in the maze. If the pause occurs in the middle of a word, use underscores to separate the part of the word preceding the pause from the part of the word following the pause. Examples:

C I went :02 sledding.




C I (went :02) went sledding.




C I went sled_ :02 _ding.

Between-utterance pauses

Colons (:) are used at the beginning of the utterance line to denote a change in speaker turn. Semicolons (;) are used to denote that there is not a change in speaker turn. However, if the speakers change, e.g., the pause occurs between the examiner's question and the student's response, then SALT recognizes a change in speaker turn regardless of whether the pause line begins with a colon or a semicolon. Examples:

Pause with a change in speaker turn (and a change in speaker).

Format options
    : :sec
    :sec
    : min:sec
    :min:sec

	

E What did you do over winter break?

: :04

C I went sledding.




Pause between utterances of same speaker - same turn.

Format options
    ; :sec
    ;sec
    ; min:sec
    ;min:sec

	

C Over break>

;05

C I went sledding over break.


Pause between utterances of same speaker - forces change in turn.	

C I like to draw pictures.

:08  { bell rang }

C How much longer do I have to talk?


Untimed pause - forces change in turn.	

C I like to draw pictures.

:   { bell rang }

C How much longer do I have to talk?

Spelling Conventions and Unintelligible Speech

Spelling Conventions

It is important to follow spelling conventions when transcribing so there is consistency within and across transcripts. Different spellings of the same word will count as different words when the language sample is analyzed. There are also conventions for filled pauses, "yes" and "no" words, and other forms.

Abbreviations	Periods are not legal word characters. Abbreviated words should either be written out or left as an abbreviation but without the period, e.g., MR, MISTER, MRS, DR
Filled Pause Words	AH, EH, ER, HM, HMM, MM, UH, UM, and any word with the code [FP]
Yes Words	OK, AHA, MHM, UHHUH, YEAH, YEP, YES
No Words	NO, AHAH, MHMH, UHUH, NAH, NOPE
Numbers	21, TWENTY-ONE, 8, EIGHT
Clock time	Do not use colons when typing clock time because it will be interpreted as a pause. Type out the words connected with an underscore, e.g., EIGHT_THIRTY, NINE_O'CLOCK
Counting and "spelled" words	1_2_3_4_5,  C_A_T
Concatenatives	BETCHA,COULDA, GONNA, GOTTA, HAFTA, LIKETA, LOOKIT, MUSTA, OUGHTA, OUTTA, SHOULDA, SPOSTA, TRYNTA, USETA, WANNA, WHATCHA, WOULDA
Sounds with specific meaning	HMM, HUH (question or affirmation), IDK (intoned I don't know), UHOH (something is wrong), SHH or %SHH
Other spellings	AIN'T, ALOT, ATTA, NOONE, OH, OOH, OOP, OOPS, OOPSY

Unintelligible Speech

Unintelligible speech is marked by using an "X".  A single "X" is used to mark a single unintelligible word or sound. Two "XX" are used to mark an unintelligible segment. Three "XXX" are used to mark a complete utterance that is unintelligible. Unintelligible words or segments must consist entirely of X's. Examples:

Unintelligible word	C I went to X to make a bear.
Unintelligible segment	C I went XX bear.
Unintelligible utterance	C XXX.
Not unintelligible	C I went to mXX a bear.

Nonverbal utterances which hold communicative intent should be marked within a transcript using curly braces {  }. Examples:

C Lookit {C points to toy}.

Sound Effects

Sound effects and other non-word vocalizations are preceded with a % percent sign if they are essential to the meaning of the utterance. If they are not essential to the meaning of the utterance, they should be entered as comments. Examples:

C The dog went %ruff.

C The boy heard %ribbit %ribbit.

C %vroom go here.

C The dog bark/ed {C makes barking sounds}.

 In the last example, the barking sounds were not essential to the meaning of the utterance.

Overlapping Speech

Overlapping speech can be an important aspect of pragmatic language skills. Within a transcript overlapping speech is marked with angle brackets < > surrounding the speakers' overlapping speech. Example:

C I went to the store with <my grandma>.

E <Oh, Grandma>!

The overlapping speech convention can also be used if feedback is inserted within a speaker's utterance.  Use empty angle brackets to mark where the inserted words occurred. Example:

C I went to the store < > with my grandma.

E <Uhhuh>.

Parenthetical Remarks

Parenthetical remarks are captured inside of ((double parentheses)).  Parenthetical remarks are comments that are off-task from the language sample.  A speaker may ask a question during a narrative retell or insert an explanation in the middle of the utterance. Examples:

C The guy ((I forgot his name)) was the robber in the movie.




C The ((what/'s it called)) <> bit him on the nose.

E <Gopher>.




C And those are the rule/s of soccer.

C ((Is that enough))?

E Sure, thanks.

Checking for Errors

Checking for Errors

When you have finished transcribing your sample, it is important to check and fix transcription errors. This ensures the most valid analysis outcome. Click on the red check mark on the top menu bar to check the transcript for errors such as missing end punctuation or unmatched parentheses. SALT does not check for misspelled words.  

If errors are found, SALT opens a dialogue box listing the errors in the transcript. The first error is highlighted. You can edit the transcript for errors. Click the next arrow to move to the next error.
