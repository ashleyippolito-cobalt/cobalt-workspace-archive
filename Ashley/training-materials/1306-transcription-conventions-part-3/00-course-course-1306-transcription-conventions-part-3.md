Gestures and nonverbal turns

Nonverbal utterances are communicative gestures or vocalizations that contribute to the speaker's turn. Gestures often represent a person's attempt to take a turn in conversation. Nodding in agreement, shaking head "no", shrugging shoulders, pointing, grunting, and following directives are possible examples. Gestures are omitted from word counts in analyses but marking them allows them to be counted as speaker turns in SALT calculations.

Marking Gestures

Nonverbal content within utterances are marked using a set of braces { } to enclose nonverbal actions that support a conversational turn and occur within utterances. For example,
C I want that one {C points to cake}.

Marking Nonverbal Turns

Nonverbal content as its own conversational turn is also transcribed using a set of braces { }. However, the gesture is given its own utterance line with the speaker identified. For example,
E Where is your book?
C {picks up book}.
E Where is your truck?
C {points to truck}.

Nonverbal Interaction and Audio Tapes

Note that if you are transcribing the language sample from an audio tape, most nonverbal interaction is lost unless the examiner makes notes on the side or verbalizes what the child did.

Overlapping Speech and Feedback

When two or more speakers speak at the same time, their utterances are entered separately because the transcript is linear. The overlapping speech is marked, however, because it may indicate a discourse problem.

Use angle brackets to mark overlapping speech

All words that are spoken concurrently are enclosed in angle brackets. Final punctuation marks are still required to end the utterance. For example,
E Do you want to see <the book now>?
C <I want to> open the book.

Overlapping speech in middle of utterance

The overlapping speech may occur in the middle of an utterance. Just mark the overlapping segments and continue transcribing the utterance. For example,
C I like that book <because it/'s about> ghosts.
E <Tell me about it>.

Overlapping speech within or around mazes

Example,
C On Saturday I went (um <to um) to> my friend/z house.
E <Where>?

Marking feedback as overlapping speech

Sometimes one speaker interjects words such as "uhhuh" or "mhm" as feedback to encourage another speaker to continue speaking. If these words overlap the other speaker's words, then mark them as you would any other overlap. For example,
C My friend <got to> go home early today.
E <Mhm>.

However, if such feedback is given in the middle of another person's utterance but does not overlap the speaker's words, use empty brackets to indicate the location of the feedback. This representation allows for a more accurate representation of the utterance count within a conversational turn. For example,
C I have two cat/s and < > some fish.
E <Mhm>.

Parenthetical Remarks

A parenthetical remark is a word or clause that has been added by the speaker as an explanation or comment. They can occur in the middle of an utterance or by themselves as stand alone utterances. To code parenthetical remarks, enclose them in double parentheses. Parenthetical remarks should be coded if 1) they occur in the middle of an utterance and do not contribute to the meaning of the utterance, or 2) they occur as stand alone utterances but do not contribute to the content of the sample. Unless coded, parenthetical remarks are included in the calculation of language measures such as the mean length of utterance (MLU) and number of different words (NDW). Note that you have the option of whether to include or exclude parenthetical remarks when analyzing the transcript (Setup menu --> Word Base). By default, they are excluded. Also note that, even if excluded from the analysis of the transcript, parenthetical remarks are always included when counting all the words in the sample.

Parenthetical remarks which occur in the middle of an utterance

When a parenthetical remark occurs in the middle of the utterance and interferes with the rest of the utterance, enclose it in double parentheses. For example,
C The boy ((I can/'t remember his name)) left the house.

C Then the ((what/'s that call/ed)) < > gopher bit him on the nose.
E <gopher>.

C Then the ((what/'s that call/ed)) < > bit him on the nose.
E <gopher>.

Parenthetical remarks which occur as stand alone utterances

When a parenthetical remark occurs as a stand alone utterance, you need to decide whether or not you want that utterance included when analyzing the sample. If included, these utterances will contribute to the length of the sample in terms of number of utterances and number of words. And the contents of the utterances will be included when calculating language measures such as number of different words (NDW).

For conversational samples, you may want these utterances included as they occur naturally in conversation. For example,
C Last weekend I went to my grandma/z house.
 ; :02
C I forgot what I was go/ing to say.
E What did you do at your grandma/z?

For story retell and expository samples, however, you may not want them included unless they contribute to the contents of the narrative. For example,
C ((I skip/ed a page)).
C ((Where was I))?

Only enclose parenthetical remarks with double parentheses if you want them excluded from the analyses.

Omitted Words and Bound Morphemes

You should mark omissions because they may be an indication of utterance formulation problems. An omission occurs when one or more words or bound morphemes that are obligatory for grammatical correctness are absent. For example, if the speaker says, "His name Larry", the copula "is" should be marked as an omission. On the other hand, if the examiner asks, "What are you doing?" and the speaker responds "Eating", there isn't an omission because no obligatory content has been left out.

Once marked, omissions can be counted and called up later for further analysis. They are not included in calculations such as "mean length of utterance", "number of different words", or "words per minute".

Omitted Words

The asterisk symbol (*) is used to indicate an omitted word. At the point in the transcript where the word was omitted, type an asterisk followed by the omitted word. There should be no blank spaces between the asterisk and the omitted word. For example,

WHAT WAS SAID
What is this?
This a cookie.
What do you want to do with it?
I want eat it.	TRANSCRIPTION
E  What is this?
C  This *is a cookie.
E  What do you want to do with it?
C  I want *to eat it.

Omitted Bound Morphemes

A slash followed by an asterisk is used to indicate the omission of a bound morpheme in obligatory context. Type the slash, the asterisk, and the missing bound morphemes at the point in the transcript where they've been omitted. For example,

WHAT WAS SAID
Where does the boy go?
The boy go here.
Then what happened?
The frog jump off.	TRANSCRIPTION
E  Where does the boy go?
C  The boy go/*3s here.
E  Then what happen/ed?
C  The frog jump/*ed off.

Omitted Contractions: Words vs Bound Morphemes

Omitted contractions may be transcribed in two ways, as an omission of a word or as an omission of a bound morpheme. It doesn't make much difference which way you mark this type of omission since omissions are not included in any of the calculations based on words or morphemes. The important thing is to mark that the omission occurred. For example,

WHAT WAS SAID
He going home.	TRANSCRIPTION
C  He *is go/ing home.
OR
C  He/*'s go/ing home.

Omitted Word Root/Bound Morpheme Combinations

If the word root is omitted, all bound morphemes located after the word root are also omitted, whether or not they are explicitly marked. For example,

WHAT WAS SAID
One more then done.	TRANSCRIPTION
C  One more then *we/'re done.
OR
C   One more then *we/'re done.
OR
C  One more then *we/*'re done.


Omitted Something - Not Sure What

With some omissions, it's difficult to know what the speaker intended to say. In these cases, you should make a "best guess" at what omission makes the most sense in the context of the sample. Use the following guidelines to help with these decisions: 1) use omissions conservatively; do not over-penalize the speaker, 2) choose words or bound morphemes that are representative of the speaker's ability, 3) use a generic part of speech, e.g., *verb, if you know the part of speech that was omitted but not the actual word(s), and 4) if there are more than two omitted words or bound morphemes, use the [EU] code at the end of the utterance. Consider these examples where there are different options for marking omissions:

WHAT WAS SAID
And then said, "Oh I forgot to mention that".	TRANSCRIPTION
C  And then *he said, "Oh I forgot to mention that".
OR
C  And then *Dr_DeSoto said, "Oh, I forgot to mention that".
WHAT WAS SAID
It taste good.	TRANSCRIPTION
C  It taste/*3s good.
OR
C  It taste/*ed good.
WHAT WAS SAID
There was a girl named Goldilocks went into the house.	TRANSCRIPTION
C  There was a girl named Goldilocks *who went into the house .
OR
C  There was a girl named Goldilocks.
C  *She went into the house.
If you can tell the part of speech that's missing, but not the actual word(s), you can mark the omission as in the following examples:
WHAT WAS SAID
And then went home.
And he the frog.
He laughing and laughing.	TRANSCRIPTION
C  And then *SUBJ went home.
C  And he *VERB the frog.
C  He *AUX laugh/ing and laugh/ing .
There are limits to how much you should "fix" the speaker's utterance. If there are more than two omitted words or bound morphemes, or if you don't have a good idea of what was omitted, flag the utterance with the error code [EU]. The [EU] code indicates an utterance-level error. Coded utterances can be counted and called up later for further analysis.
WHAT WAS SAID
What is Sammy doing?
He book.	TRANSCRIPTION
E  What is Sammy do/ing?
C  He book [EU].

Using Bracketed Codes

Codes can be attached to any word or utterance in your transcript to mark features for which there isn't a transcription convention.

Acceptable Codes

A code consists of characters enclosed within square brackets. When you create a custom code do not use a blank space within the code and never split the code between lines. Avoid using symbols which have special meaning in SALT such as transcript-entry and search symbols. In particular, avoid using the @ sign and the = sign as their usage will generate a warning message in SALT. For example,

ACCEPTABLE CODES
[EP:pronoun]
[TopicInit]

	

UNACCEPTABLE CODES
[EP pronoun]
[LEX=FROG]

Word Codes

There are two types of codes - word codes and utterance codes. A word code is any code that is attached to the end of a word without any space between the word and the code. There's no limit to the number of words that may be coded or to the number of codes that may be attached to each word. Word codes are used to mark features of specific words. For example,

    C The big frog were[EW:was] mad.    word-level error
    C Then he[Ref] push/ed him[NoRef] off.    coding pronoun referencing

Utterance Codes

An utterance code is any code that is not attached to a word. They may occur anywhere in the utterance before the end-of-utterance punctuation mark. There's no limit to the number of utterance codes that may be inserted. For example,

    C He book [EU].    utterance-level error
    E What are your plan/s for the weekend [TopicInit]?    utterance which initiates a new topic
    C And [POINT:car] [POINT:garage] here.    coding gestures

Using Codes to Mark Errors

SALT recognizes a special category of codes called "error codes". These codes are only used to mark errors in syntax and morphology. They are not used to mark errors in semantics or pragmatics.

Default error codes - there are default error codes which can be edited to suit your purposes. They include all codes which begin with "EO" (overgeneralization errors), "EP" (pronoun errors), "EW" (other word-level errors), and "EU" (utterance-level errors). You can edit the list of codes SALT recognizes as error codes using Setup menu --> Lists --> Current Code Lists

Although all codes are included in most code summaries, there are a few places, such as the Standard Measures Report, where only the error codes are summarized. There are four error codes that are used consistently when transcribing samples for the SALT reference databases - [EO] for overgeneralization errors, [EP] for pronoun errors, [EW] for other word-level errors, and [EU] for utterance-level errors.

 Overgeneralization errors, the [EO] code

There are two steps to marking overgeneralization errors.

Step 1: Insert the code [EO] at the end of the overgeneralized word.

The code [EO] is the standard word-level error code used to mark overgeneralization errors. The correct word is put inside the brackets following a colon. There is no space between the word and the code. For example,

C He falled[EO:fell].
C There were deers[EO:deer] in the woods.
C That/'s hises[EO:his].
C The next day the boy woked[EO:woke] up.

Step 2: Identify the root form.

In the preceding examples, the overgeneralized words result in new words, e.g., "falled", "deers", "hises", and "woked". To avoid overly inflating the number of different words (NDW), these words should be identified as instances of the root form, e.g., "fall", "deer", "his", and "wake". To identify the root form, follow each overgeneralized word with a vertical bar '|' (located above the backslash character on most keyboards) and the root form as shown in these examples:

C He falled|fall[EO:fell].
C There were deers|deer[EO:deer] in the woods.
C That/'s hises|his[EO:his].
C The next day the boy woked|wake[EO:woke] up.

Notice that the bound morphemes in the overgeneralized words are not marked because we don't want to give the speaker credit for a bound morpheme that should not have been used. Also notice that, in the third example, the overgeneralization error "hises" is also a pronoun error. We made the decision, in this case, to mark the error as an overgeneralization rather than a pronoun error.

Special case: duplication of the bound morpheme

Consider the following example where the speaker repeated the bound morpheme "ed":

C Then the dog droppeded it.

Although this is not a typical example of overgeneralization (and you might successfully argue that it is not overgeneralization) we use the [EO] code to mark it.

C Then the dog droppeded|drop/ed[EO:dropped] it.

Since the speaker correctly used the past tense bound morpheme but then duplicated it, the speaker is given credit for one of the uses by including the bound morpheme with the root form.

 Pronoun errors, the [EP] code

The code [EP] is the standard word-level error code used to mark pronoun errors. The correct pronoun is put inside the brackets following a colon. For example,

C The big frog was mad at theys[EP:them].
C How do them[EP:they] sit down? 
C  Him[EP:he] was sick.

Notice that each error code is attached to the specific word that is used incorrectly. There is no space between the word and the code. In the first three examples, the pronoun that should have been used is put inside the brackets following a colon. For the SALT reference database, errors involving personal pronouns, possessive pronouns, and reflexive pronouns were marked with the [EP] code..

 Other word-level errors, the [EW] code

The code [EW] is the standard word-level error code used to mark other words that are used incorrectly. The correct word, if known, is put inside the brackets following a colon. For example,

C The big frog were[EW:was] mad.
C How does[EW:do] they sit down?
C And then the turtle was of[EW]  better.

Notice that each error code is attached to the specific word that is used incorrectly. There is no space between the word and the code. In the first three examples, the word that should have been used is put inside the brackets following a colon. In the last example, the word "of" is an extraneous word; there is no correct substitution.

 Utterance-level errors, the [EU] code

The standard utterance-level error code is [EU]. This error code is used to mark errors that cannot be associated with a specific word. The [EU] code is inserted between the last word and the ending punctuation mark. For example,

C He book [EU].
C And they came to stop/ed [EU].
C They scream for everywhere [EU].
C Why this part does/n't open [EU]?
C He was have/ing a frog [EU].
C He was a tree [EU].
C The kid tell (the) everything to him happen [EU].

General Rule: Do not mark more than two omissions and/or word errors in an utterance. Instead, mark the entire utterance as having a problem by inserting the utterance code [EU] at the end of the utterance. Also, do not mark any utterance containing unintelligible segments as [EU] since you don't know if the unintelligible segments would have made the utterance acceptable.

 Designing your own error codes

You can also devise your own set of error codes. Perhaps you would like more specific word-level error codes. If you want your codes to be summarized as error codes, either be sure they begin with the letter "E" (SALT 2012) or use the Setup menu in SALT to change the list of error codes (SALT 16 and SALT 18). In the following examples, the code [EV] is used for verb errors.

C The big frog were[EV:was] mad.  verb error
C How does[EV:do] they sit down? verb error

Devising Your Own Coding Scheme

You can devise your own codes to analyze any feature of the language sample that you are interested in. After the sample is transcribed you can change the Analysis Set to include or exclude coded utterances and then run analysis measures based on specifically selected utterances. You can also change the Word Base to include or exclude coded words from the analyses. Unique coding is also useful for tracking progress made in therapy with repeat samples coded for specific features. There are endless possibilities for unique coding schemes since the coding is so flexible. Below are just a few examples of how to implement a unique coding scheme.

Example 1: coding student's responses to questions. First you would construct an appropriate set of codes. In this example, three utterance-level codes are used: [AR] for appropriate response, [IR] for inappropriate response, and [NR] for no response. The "appropriate response" and "inappropriate response" codes are inserted at the end of the student's utterance which follows the examiner's question. The "no response" code is inserted at the end of the examiner's question to which the student did not respond.

E Do you have any plan/s for the weekend?
C No [AR].
E Did you know there/'s a track meet here on Saturday [NR]?
: 0:05
E Do you know anyone on the track team?
C I don't like track [IR].

Example 2: marking utterances for African American English (AAE) dialect. Suppose you suspect that a speaker's use of dense AAE dialect is affecting measures such as MLU and NDW. The easiest way to find out would be to a) flag all the utterances containing AAE dialect, b) generate analysis measures based only on those flagged (coded) utterances, and c) compare the results with measures generated using all utterances. To illustrate this coding, the following utterances are coded as [D] if they contain AAE dialect:

C They be happy about the new baby [D].
C They walk/ing the baby [D].
C They want/ed to name him all different kind of name/s [D].
C Maybe Lance was a good name.

Example 3: mark code switching as ESL students acquire English. The following utterances are taken from a native Spanish-speaking student asked to retell a story in English. Each code-switched word is marked with the word-level code [CS]. Once coded, the SALT software can be used to count the number of occurrences of the [CS] code, and pull up lists of the coded words and utterances.

C This cuento[CS] es[CS] about the dog and the frog.
C La[CS] rana[CS] was gone.
C The frog was gone.

Example 4: mark non-specific vocabulary. In the following examples, the target speaker's uses of non-specific labels such as "thing" and "stuff" are marked with the word code [NV].

C I use that thing[NV] to catch the ball.
C Give me the green (um um) stuff[NV].

Example 5: mark lexicon differences. Suppose the target speaker is retelling a story and uses the a vocabulary word that is not optimal, such as "bush" when talking about the "tree". You would not use the error codes discussed in the previous section because error codes are used only for errors of syntax and morphology. Instead, you may want to develop your own coding scheme to flag other types of errors you are interested in tracking. The following examples illustrate using the word code [LEX] to flag this choice in lexicon.

C He saw an owl in a bush[LEX:tree].

C The boy fell into the river[LEX:pond].

Defining your own codes: Although SALT needs to know which codes are error codes (see previous section), it does not need to know the other codes used in the sample. Just be consistent in their use so that you can compare performance within and between samples. Consider inserting plus lines near the beginning of your transcript to define the codes you use. For example,

$ Child, Examiner
+ Gender: F
+ CA: 6;8
+ Context: Con
+ [EW] = word-level error
+ [EU] = utterance-level error
+ [CS] = code switching
+ [NV] = non-specific vocabulary
+ [LEX] = lexicon
