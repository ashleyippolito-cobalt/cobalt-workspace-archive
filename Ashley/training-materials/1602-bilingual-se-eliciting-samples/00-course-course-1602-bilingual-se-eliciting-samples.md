Course 1602: Bilingual SE - Eliciting Samples
Collapse all

One of the most powerful analysis tools built into SALT is the ability to compare an individual language sample to age or grade-matched peers selected from the reference databases. In order for the comparison to be valid, however, the language samples should be elicited following the same protocol as was used to collect the database samples. In this course you will learn about SALT's Bilingual Spanish/English and Monolingual Spanish reference databases and how to collect comparable samples.

Skills and Equipment

What skills are needed to elicit the samples?

One doesn't have to be a speech language pathologist to elicit a language sample. The examiner should, however, have experience talking with children. The first few minutes of the language sample interaction are critical. If the examiner fails to establish a comfortable rapport with the speaker, the resulting language sample will be strained and will lack the necessary spontaneity to function as a valid index of the speaker's productive language performance. View eliciting good language samples for suggestions.

There are different ways to provide the story model and record the student's story. You may have the student seated next to you or you may be eliciting the sample virtually. You may be using SALT's Online Story Elicitation program (available as an annual subscription on the SALT web site) or providing the directions and story model yourself. Both methods should result in similar outcomes (Manning, et al., 2020, Brennan, et al., 2004). 

If you are providing the directions and story model yourself, you should be proficient in the target language (Spanish or English). Otherwise, you should be proficient enough in the target language to be an engaged listener and to prompt the child as needed. 

What equipment is needed?

The equipment needed depends on the method you are using to elicit the samples.

Option 1: Use SALT's Online Story Elicitation Bilingual Spanish/English & Monolingual Spanish program (in-person or virtual session).

If this in an in-person session, use the recorder built into the program. If eliciting the sample virtually, use an alternate recorder, e.g., your phone, the recorder built into Zoom or other software for hosting virtual meetings. All directions and story images are included in the program.

Option 2: Provide the directions and story models yourself (in-person session).

Equipment list:

An audio or video recorder. Use a digital recorder for better sound quality and more playback options.
The specific story book used for the elicitation.
The elicitation protocol and story scripts.
Audio recording of the Spanish and English scripts (alternate elicitation protocol). They are available from the Resources section of the SALT web site.

Type of Sample to Elicit

The goal when eliciting a sample is to have the speaker use his own words and form his own phrases without examiner intervention aiding or reducing productivity. A representative sample must have reliability, which is the degree to which repeated samples are similar in content. This means that the elicitation of language samples is done the same way each time so that the speaker's language sample can be compared to language samples from other speakers or to language samples collected at different points in time.

How do I decide what type of language sample to elicit?

There are several things to consider when choosing the type of language sample to elicit.

Sampling Context: Conversational samples target pragmatic language skills for social interaction and classroom participation. These skills include: turn taking, topic maintenance, and responding to contingent speech in conversation. Narratives and expositories are often considered to have the most relevance for school performance and can be tied to state or district standards for oral language. Narrative samples provide information about the speaker's oral communication skills which are required for learning to read, maintaining a story structure, relating the order of events, and describing the relationship of story characters. Expository samples target the speaker's organizational skills needed to describe, provide information, or inform the listener clearly and logically.


Speaker's Age: Choose a sampling context which is appropriate for the speaker's age and/or grade in school.


How will you interpret the samples? Will you be using the SALT reference databases or tracking the speaker's progress in intervention? The SALT project has developed narrative elicitation protocols to standardize the language sampling process for English language learners in grades K - 3 and for monolingual Spanish speakers in grades 1 - 3. Following these protocols will allow for comparison with age or grade-matched samples selected from SALT's Bilingual Spanish/English and Monolingual Spanish reference databases. This will give you an accurate assessment of the speaker's productive language in both languages. You can choose to elicit samples using one or both of the narrative tasks described below.

Bilingual Spanish/English or Monolingual Spanish

SALT has reference databases for English-fluent speakers, for English learners (EL) whose primary language is Spanish, and for monolingual Spanish speakers. Use the following guidelines to determine which database should be used for comparison:

Databases for English-fluent speakers
The speaker is fluent in English.
Bilingual Spanish/English reference databases
The EL student is able to produce at least one complete and intelligible utterance in both Spanish and English.
Monolingual Spanish reference database
The speaker is fluent in Spanish but unable to meet the criteria to be considered bilingual (above).

Bilingual Story Retell Task

The story retell task requires the student to listen to a story while observing the story's picture sequence, and then retell the same story. This task is repeated for both Spanish and English to provide the opportunity for direct comparison of language proficiency. This narrative task provides the student with optimum support to retell the story in each language - with an audio model provided by the examiner and the visual support of the story book.

The SALT story retell databases contain both English and Spanish samples from over 4,000 English language learners in kindergarten through 3rd grade using the following wordless picture books by Mercer Mayer:

FWAY: Frog, Where are You (1969)
FGTD: Frog Goes to Dinner (1974)
FOHO: Frog on His Own (1973).

Elicitation Method

Option 1 (In-person or virtual session): Use SALT's Online Story Elicitation Bilingual Spanish/English & Monolingual Spanish program. Select the category (Bilingual Spanish/English), the target language (Spanish or English), and the story (FWAY, FGTD, or FOHO). Follow the prompts. There is a YouTube video showing how to elicit a Spanish sample in the Summary section of this course. 

Option 2 (In-person session): Have the speaker seated next to you with the book in front of you. Provide the story model while looking at the pictures in the book. There are two ways to present the story model. Both methods should result in similar outcomes (Kim, 2016). Look through the book with the child while you tell a story loosely following the story script or play an audio recording of the story.

Bilingual Unique Story Task

With the unique story task, the student looks through the pictures in a book and is then asked to tell the story. No model of this story is given. It is assumed, however, that the student has previously produced a story retell and is familiar with the notion of telling a story based on the pictures. If not, the examiner should begin this task by modeling a different frog story.

The SALT unique story database contains both English and Spanish samples from almost 500 English language learners in kindergarten through 3rd grade using the wordless picture book One Frog Too Many by Mercer and Marianna Mayer (1975).

Read the Bilingual SE Unique Story database description and the elicitation protocol. 

Elicitation Method

Option 1 (In-person or virtual session): Use SALT's Online Story Elicitation Bilingual Spanish/English & Monolingual Spanish program. Select the category (Bilingual Spanish/English), the target language (Spanish or English), and the story (One Frog Too Many). Follow the prompts. There is a YouTube video showing how to elicit a Spanish sample in the Summary section of this course. 

Option 2 (In-person session): Have the speaker seated next to you with the book in front of you. Have the speaker quietly look through the pictures of the book. Then ask the speaker to use the book to retell the story in his or her own words.
