## MODULE 1: Why Language Sample Analysis?
**Target length:** 10 minutes (~1,300 words)

---

Welcome to the SALT Training Series. Whether you're new to language sample analysis or returning to update your skills, this series is built around one goal: making you fast, accurate, and confident with SALT from day one.

[pause]

Let's start with the fundamental question: **why collect a language sample?**

[screen: slide — "Standardized Test" vs. "Language Sample" comparison table]

Standardized tests are efficient. You get a score, you compare it to a norm. But here's what a test can't tell you: how a child actually uses language to communicate — how they construct sentences, organize their ideas, tell a story, explain a concept. Those skills are invisible on a test. They're visible in a language sample.

[pause]

Language sample analysis — LSA — gives us a window into a speaker's **productive language ability**. Not what they can do under artificial test conditions, but what they produce spontaneously. And that distinction matters enormously for diagnosis and for intervention planning.

[screen: slide — three reasons, appearing one at a time]

**Three reasons LSA belongs in your clinical toolkit.**

One: **ecological validity.** A language sample reflects how a child actually communicates — in conversation, in storytelling, in explaining ideas. That's the language that matters for school and social success.

Two: **sensitivity.** LSA captures differences that standardized tests miss — syntactic complexity, vocabulary diversity, narrative organization. These are often where children with language disorders show the most need.

Three: **equity.** For children from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds — including bilingual learners — standardized tests are often normed on populations that don't reflect your client. Language samples, especially with bilingual norms, provide a more valid and equitable assessment pathway.

[pause]

[screen: SALT platform — brief overview screenshot of the editor and report]

This series is built around the **SALT platform** — a modern, web-based tool that handles the full language sampling workflow: session setup, transcription, analysis, and scoring. Everything you'll learn in this series maps directly to what you'll do in the platform. By the end, you'll be able to take a speaker from recorded sample to clinical report entirely within the platform.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "What you'll learn in this series" — module roadmap]

Here's the roadmap. We'll start with how to choose and collect the right sample. Then we'll create a session in the platform, transcribe using SALT conventions with the platform's built-in editor, generate and interpret a SALT report, apply the scoring schemes for narrative, expository, and persuasive language, and handle bilingual assessment. We'll close with a full case from start to finish.

You'll meet Carter throughout this series — a 5th grader referred for language concerns. His case will anchor each module and show you how the pieces connect in real clinical practice.

## MODULE 2: Choosing the Right Sample
**Target length:** 12 minutes (~1,560 words)

---

Before you open the platform and create a session, you need to make one critical decision: **what kind of sample do you want to collect?**

[pause]

[screen: slide — "The Four SALT Sampling Contexts"]

SALT has four sampling contexts: **conversation, narration, exposition, and persuasion.** Each one taps into different aspects of language — and each is appropriate for different ages and clinical goals. Choosing the wrong context means you may not capture the language you're trying to assess.

[pause]

**Conversation** targets pragmatic language — turn-taking, topic maintenance, responding to contingent speech. Appropriate starting in preschool. Use it when your concern is social communication.

[pause]

**Narration** — story retell — is the most commonly used context. It taps directly into skills linked to literacy: story structure, sequencing, describing relationships between characters and events. Appropriate starting in preschool and sensitive to language disorder across a wide age range.

[pause]

**Exposition** targets organizational language — the ability to describe, explain, and inform. This is academic language: the language of classroom instruction. Appropriate starting in 5th grade.

[pause]

**Persuasion** targets argument structure — claiming a position, providing evidence, considering counterarguments. Appropriate starting in 6th grade, and closely aligned to Common Core oral language expectations.

[screen: slide — table: Context / Starting Grade / What It Measures]

Quick reference: Conversation and Narration start in preschool. Exposition starts in 5th grade. Persuasion in 6th. Fable narration in 8th.

[pause]

[screen: decision flowchart — "What sampling context should I use?"]

How do you decide? Ask three questions.

First: what's the referral concern? Social pragmatics — conversation. Narrative organization — narration. Academic language — exposition or persuasion depending on grade.

Second: what's the child's age and grade? Match the context to developmental readiness. Don't give an expository task to a 2nd grader.

Third: do you want to compare to the SALT reference database? If yes, you need to use the same context and elicitation protocol used to build those databases. That's what makes the comparison valid.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Why Elicitation Protocol Consistency Matters"]

This brings us to **elicitation protocols**. SALT has standardized protocols for each context. Two clinicians following the same protocol with the same child should get comparable samples. And if you follow the same protocol used for the reference databases, your sample can be meaningfully compared to those norms.

[pause]

The principles are straightforward. You want the child using their own words, forming their own sentences, without you scaffolding their output. Keep your own language neutral. Use contingent responses — "uh huh," "okay," "tell me more." Avoid yes/no questions. Avoid content-specific prompts. Let the child lead.

[pause]

If a child pauses for more than five seconds, a neutral prompt is fine: "What happens next?" or "Keep going." Beyond that, step back.

[screen: slide — "Narration Protocol Example — Frog, Where Are You?"]

For a story retell, the standard stimulus is a wordless picture book. "Frog, Where Are You?" is the most commonly used. You look through it together, then you say: "Now I want you to tell me the story using the pictures. Start at the beginning and tell me the whole story." Then you let them go.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Common Elicitation Mistakes"]

Three mistakes I see most often.

Prompting too much. Every content prompt you give scaffolds the child's output and inflates their apparent ability. Keep prompts neutral and infrequent.

Stopping too soon. A valid sample needs at least 50 utterances. Give it time.

Choosing the wrong context. A 3rd grader given an expository prompt may produce a sample that looks disordered — because the task wasn't developmentally appropriate, not because the child has a disorder. Context mismatch is a diagnostic risk.

[pause]

[screen: platform screenshot — Sample Type picker in NewSessionSalt]

When you create a session in the platform — which we'll cover in the next module — you'll select the sample type from a picker that shows all four contexts plus specialized options like Fable and Play. The database recommendation updates automatically based on what you select. The decisions you make in elicitation connect directly to what the platform does downstream.

[pause]

Module 2 summary: choose your context based on referral concern, age, and whether you need database comparison. Follow the SALT protocol consistently. Minimize examiner influence. Aim for 50+ utterances.

## MODULE 4: C-Unit Segmentation
**Target length:** 10 minutes (~1,300 words)

---

Of all the transcription skills in SALT, C-unit segmentation is the one people find most challenging at first. That's because speech doesn't arrive pre-segmented. A child talks in a continuous stream — and your job is to divide that stream into units the platform can analyze.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "C-Unit Definition"]

A **C-unit — communication unit** — is an independent clause and all its modifiers, including subordinate clauses.

One complete thought. One main verb and everything attached to it.

[pause]

Why C-units specifically? Because they're syntax-based, which means different clinicians applying the same rules arrive at consistent segmentation. And because the SALT reference databases were transcribed using C-units — so if you want a valid database comparison, you need to match that method.

[pause]

[screen: platform screenshot — editor with WorkflowPanel open to Step 2]

In the platform, the WorkflowPanel's Step 2 — Segmentation — guides you through this process. The C-unit count in the editor footer updates live as you work. You can see your segmentation taking shape in real time.

[pause]

[screen: slide — color-coded transcript examples]

Let me walk through the core rule with examples.

*"I thought the frog jumped."* — **One C-unit.** Main clause plus subordinate clause — but one complete thought.

*"I went to my friend's house this weekend and we went to Big Al's to bowl."* — **Two C-units.** The coordinating conjunction "and" signals a new independent clause.

```
C I went to my friend/z house this weekend.
C And we went to Big_Al's to bowl.
```

[pause]

[screen: slide — "The Conjunction Rule"]

**The conjunction rule is the key.**

**Coordinating conjunctions** — and, but, so, or — usually signal a new C-unit. Segment here. Note: "then" is not a coordinating conjunction for this purpose — it does not signal a new C-unit on its own.

**Subordinating conjunctions** — because, that, when, where, while, unless — do NOT signal a new C-unit. Keep these in the same utterance.

[pause]

So: *"I fell because I wasn't looking"* is **one C-unit.** The "because" clause is subordinate — it can't stand alone.

But: *"I fell and I wasn't looking where I was going"* is **two C-units.** The "and" joins two independent clauses.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Conditional Structures + Edge Cases"]

**Conditional structures** — if/then, either/or — stay together. *"If we don't hurry, we'll be late"* is one C-unit.

**Fragments** count as a C-unit if the speaker's intonation signals a complete thought. *"A big one."* in response to a question about a dog — that's a C-unit.

**Elliptical answers** — *"Because he was scared."* — are C-units. Short, fragment-like, but they're communicative responses.

**Sentence tags** — "I guess," "you know," "I mean" — do NOT get their own C-unit. They stay attached.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Platform: MarkPanel Terminators"]

In the platform editor, every C-unit line needs an ending punctuation mark. The MarkPanel's Terminators group gives you one-click access to all of them: period, exclamation, question mark, tilde for examiner prompts, caret for interrupted utterances, greater-than for abandoned utterances. Use these to close each C-unit line as you segment.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Practice Example"]

Here's a longer example. A child narrating:

*"And um the boy was looking for the frog and he called and called but the frog didn't come and so he got really sad and then he went to bed."*

Segmented:
```
C And [um] the boy was look/ing for the frog.
C And he call/ed and call/ed.
C But the frog did/n't come.
C And so he got really sad.
C And then he went to bed.
```

Five C-units from one run-on stream. Each coordinating conjunction becomes a segmentation point.

[pause]

Module 4 in review: a C-unit is one independent clause and all its modifiers. Coordinating conjunctions signal new C-units; subordinating conjunctions don't. Fragments and elliptical answers count when they're communicative. Use the MarkPanel terminators to close each line.

## MODULE 5: Transcription Conventions in the Editor
**Target length:** 15 minutes (~1,950 words)

---

Transcription conventions are the markup layer of a SALT transcript. They're the codes that allow the platform to count, measure, and analyze beyond just the words. In this module we'll cover all the essential conventions — and show you how the platform's MarkPanel makes applying them faster and less error-prone.

[pause]

[screen: platform screenshot — editor with MarkPanel open and color coding ON]

Before we get into the conventions themselves, let me show you two features that make this whole process more manageable.

First: the **MarkPanel** — the left panel in the editor. It's organized into groups by convention type, with buttons for every code you'll use. You can click a button with your cursor on a word to apply the convention to that word, or click with no selection to insert the raw code at your cursor position. No memorizing syntax — just click.

Second: the **color coding toggle** in the toolbar. Turn it on and your transcript lights up: speaker labels in blue, mazes in amber, bound morphemes in purple, error codes in red, omissions in pink, unintelligibles in orange. Turn it on periodically while you transcribe to visually audit your work. If something should be colored and it isn't, you missed a code.

[pause]

Now let's go through each convention group.

[screen: slide — "Bound Morphemes — the Purple Group"]

**Bound morphemes.** This is the one that trips people up most, so let's spend real time on it.

SALT marks a specific set of grammatical morphemes using a slash. The purpose: measuring grammatical development. But not all morphemes get marked — only **inflectional** morphemes. Here's why.

[pause]

**Inflectional morphemes** encode grammatical information — plural, possessive, tense, aspect. They never change the grammatical category of a word. "Look" and "looked" are both verbs. "Cat" and "cats" are both nouns. When a child uses an inflectional morpheme, they're encoding two meanings simultaneously: the root word, and the grammatical function.

**Derivational morphemes** create new words or change grammatical category — "read" becomes "reader," "friend" becomes "friendly." Roger Brown's foundational work on MLU showed that children typically learn derived words as whole units, not as root plus suffix. So SALT doesn't count them as separate morphemes. We mark what reflects the child's active grammatical knowledge.

[pause]

[screen: slide — The 8 Inflectional Morphemes table]

The **inflectional morphemes** SALT marks, and their codes. You'll find all of these in the purple MarkPanel group.

**/S** — plural. KITTEN/S. Do not mark irregular plurals where the root word sound changes: MICE, LEAVES, WOLVES, KNIVES, GEESE.

**/Z** — possessive. DAD/Z. Do not mark possessive pronouns: HIS, HERS, ITS, OURS, YOURS, THEIRS.

**/S/Z** — plural possessive. BABY/S/Z. Order doesn't matter — /S/Z or /Z/S both work.

**/ED** — regular past tense. JUMP/ED. Do not mark irregular past tense: WENT, SAW, RAN.

**/EN** — past participle. She has EATEN. The spot is TAKEN. Mark this when the past participle form is used.

**/ING** — progressive. LOOK/ING. Always mark this — it's straightforward. **Exception:** do NOT mark /ING when the -ing word follows a preposition (gerund after preposition): "kept on singing," "after eating," "by trying" — no slash.

**/3S** — third person singular present. She RUN/3S fast.

**/N'T** — contracted "not." DID/N'T, WAS/N'T, CAN/N'T. **Do not** slash contractions where the root word sound changes: won't, don't. Do not mark ain't.

**Contractions** are morphemes too. Standard contractions are always marked: /'s (is), /'re, /'ll, /'m, /'d, /'ve. Non-standard contractions also marked: /h's (has), /d's (does), /d'd (did), /'us. Example: He/'s leaving. Let/'us go — NOT let/'s (which would mean "let is"). **Do not** slash contractions where the root sound changes.

[pause]

**Important: /ER and /EST are not marked in SALT.** Comparative and superlative inflections are excluded from the morpheme set.

Common traps: words without a singular form — PANTS, GLASSES, WOODS, CLOTHES — do not mark /S. Time and measurement possessives do get /Z: a week/z time, three day/s/z notice. Do not mark bound morphemes on proper names or titles: Mothers_Day, Children's_Museum — no slash.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Mazes — the Amber Group"]

**Mazes.** Any portion of an utterance that disrupts forward communication — filled pauses, repetitions, revisions, false starts — goes in **parentheses**.

- Filled pause: (um), (uh)
- Repetition: (I I) went to the store
- Revision: (I went — we went) to the park
- False start: (He — the boy) climbed the tree

**Important:** "like" used as a filler — not as a simile ("she is like her dad") or estimate ("like six dogs") — is a filled pause and gets a special code: `(like[FP])`. The [FP] marks it as a filled pause specifically, which the engine tracks separately.

[pause]

Why mark mazes? The platform calculates **maze percentage** — proportion of words inside maze parentheses vs. total words. An elevated maze percentage can signal word retrieval difficulty, language formulation problems, or anxiety. It's a clinically meaningful measure that's entirely invisible without coding.

The MarkPanel's amber "Mazes" group gives you (UM)/(UH) buttons for common fillers, and a "wrap" button to surround selected text in parentheses for revisions and repetitions.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Error Codes — the Red Group"]

**Error codes.** When a speaker produces an error, mark it directly on the error word.

- **[EW]** — **extraneous word.** An extra word that makes the utterance ungrammatical, with no obvious same-part-of-speech replacement. Example: "She went for[EW] to the store." The word "for" is extra and there's no correct word to swap in — the utterance is just wrong. This is different from a revision or substitution.
- **[EU]** — utterance-level error. The error affects the whole utterance and can't be pinned to a single word.
- **[EO]** — **overgeneralization.** A child applies a regular rule to an irregular form. Format: `production|root[EO:correct]`. Examples: `He falled|fall[EO:fell].` `There were deers|deer[EO:deer] in the woods.` **Do not** mark a slash on a bound morpheme that is in error — do not write `bite/ed`; instead write `bited|bite[EO:bit]`.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Omissions — the Pink Group"]

**Omissions** are marked when a required grammatical element is absent. The asterisk is a **prefix** — it goes before the omitted word only, no closing asterisk.

Example: *"She running"* → `C She *is run/ing.`

For an omitted bound morpheme, place the asterisk after the slash: `boy/*z bed` — this tells SALT there was an omitted possessive morpheme, not an omitted word.

If two or more words are omitted, use [EU] (utterance-level error) instead of stacking multiple asterisks.

The MarkPanel's pink Omissions group inserts the asterisk prefix at cursor.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Unintelligibles — the Orange Group"]

**Unintelligible words.** When you can't make out what was said: X = one unintelligible word. XX = multiple unintelligible words. XXX = the entire utterance is unintelligible. These refer to **words**, not syllables.

The MarkPanel's orange Unintelligible group inserts X, XX, or XXX with one click.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Other Conventions — Specialized Accordion"]

A few other conventions you'll encounter. Open the **Specialized Conventions accordion** in the MarkPanel to access all of these.

**Dialectal variation [D].** When a child produces a form consistent with their dialect — not an error — mark it [D]. Note: verify this code is active in your platform version before using.

**Proper nouns and compound words.** Connect words that function as a single unit with an underscore: Big_Al's, New_York, swimming_pool. This prevents the platform from counting them as separate word types when calculating vocabulary diversity.

**Timed pauses.** Mark within-utterance pauses with a colon and duration: `:03` for a 3-second pause. Between-utterance pauses with speaker change: `;:04`.

**Overlapping speech.** Use angle brackets `< >` when two speakers talk simultaneously.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Self-Check: Three Utterances"]

Let's do a quick self-check. I'll show you an utterance and you decide how to code it.

**Utterance 1:** *"The cats were sleeping on the couch."*

[pause]

Answer: `C The cat/s were sleep/ing on the couch.` — /S for plural, /ING for progressive.

[pause]

**Utterance 2:** *"Um I don't know um she went somewhere."*

[pause]

Answer: `C (um) I do/n't know (um) she went somewhere.` — both "um"s in maze parentheses; do/n't marks the contracted "not."

[pause]

**Utterance 3:** *"He runned to the store."*

[pause]

Answer: `C He runned|run[EO:ran] to the store.` — "runned" is an overgeneralization error. Use the pipe operator to show the produced form, the root, and the [EO] code with the correct form. Do not write `run/ed` — we don't slash a morpheme that is in error.

[pause]

Module 5 in review: mark inflectional morphemes with a slash — use the purple MarkPanel group. Code mazes in **parentheses** — amber group. Flag overgeneralizations with [EO], extraneous words with [EW] — red group. Mark omissions with a prefix asterisk — pink group. Mark unintelligibles with X — orange group. Turn on color coding periodically to audit your work visually.

## MODULE 6: Generating and Reading Your SALT Report
**Target length:** 15 minutes (~1,950 words)

---

Your transcript is coded. Now it's time to turn it into clinical data. In this module we'll walk through the final steps of the WorkflowPanel, generate the report, and learn how to read what it's telling you.

[screen: platform screenshot — WorkflowPanel showing all 5 steps]

The **WorkflowPanel** on the right side of the editor guides you through five steps. You've worked through transcription, segmentation, and errors and morphology in the previous modules. Let's talk about what comes next.

**Step 4 — Extra.** This step is optional and covers the specialized scoring schemes: SI, NSS, PSS, and ESS. We'll cover those in detail in Modules 7 and 8. For now, you can skip Step 4 and come back to it after generating your initial report.

[pause]

**Step 5 — Validate and Score.** This is your final checklist before generating the report. Review it carefully: every C-unit has a terminator, morphemes are coded on target speaker lines, mazes are in parentheses, errors are flagged. The platform can catch some formatting issues, but this is your clinical review step.

[pause]

[screen: platform screenshot — Generate Report button + consent modal]

When you click **Generate Report**, the platform surfaces a consent modal before proceeding. It shows the session details — speaker, sample type, date — and asks you to check a box acknowledging that you've reviewed the transcript for accuracy and that the clinical interpretation is your responsibility.

[pause]

This isn't bureaucratic. SALT analysis is only as accurate as the transcript. The consent step exists to make explicit what was always true: the clinician owns the output.

[pause]

Check the box and proceed.

[screen: platform screenshot — ReportView]

**Reading the Report.**

The report gives you the standard SALT measures with database comparison. Let me walk through the key ones.

[pause]

[screen: annotated report — MLUw callout]

**MLUw — Mean Length of Utterance in Words.** Average number of words per C-unit. This tells you about sentence length relative to peers. A child producing short sentences relative to age-matched peers may have limited syntactic development — or may be avoiding complex structures.

[pause]

[screen: annotated report — NDW callout]

**NDW — Number of Different Word Roots.** A measure of vocabulary diversity. A child repeating the same small set of words produces a low NDW. This is one of the most sensitive markers of expressive vocabulary limitation and is frequently below average in children with language disorders.

[pause]

[screen: annotated report — Maze % callout]

**Maze Percentage.** The proportion of words produced inside maze brackets. An elevated maze percentage — especially combined with low NDW — points toward word retrieval difficulty. Carter's expository sample, which we'll look at in Module 10, shows exactly this pattern.

[pause]

[screen: annotated report — SD score column]

**Standard Deviation scores.** Every measure in the report includes an SD score relative to the database. The platform uses color coding based on these bands:

Within ±1.0 SD: typical — no flag.
Between ±1.0 and ±1.5 SD: borderline — yellow/amber flag, monitor and consider in context.
Beyond ±1.5 SD: outside typical range — red flag, clinically significant.

[pause]

An important caution: SD scores are not diagnoses. A measure flagged red needs clinical interpretation — is this consistent with other findings? Is the database comparison appropriate for this speaker? Does the elicitation context explain the result? The report informs your judgment; it doesn't replace it.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Other Key Measures"]

A few other measures worth knowing:

**TNW — Total Number of Words.** Raw productivity. A very short sample (under 50 utterances) may produce unreliable measures — this is why we emphasized sample length in Module 2.

**C-unit count.** Confirms your sample is sufficient for analysis.

**Utterance List.** A report you can generate from the report view that lists every utterance individually — useful for qualitative review, spotting error patterns, and identifying where the child's language breaks down.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Progress Monitoring"]

One of the most powerful uses of SALT in ongoing clinical work is **progress monitoring.** Collect a baseline sample at the start of an intervention cycle. ## MODULE 7: Measuring Syntactic Complexity — The Subordination Index
**Target length:** 10 minutes (~1,300 words)

---

MLU tells us how long a child's sentences are. But length isn't the same as complexity. A child can produce long utterances by stringing together simple clauses — "and then... and then... and then..." — without any syntactic sophistication. The **Subordination Index** measures what MLU misses.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "SI Formula"]

**SI = Total clauses (main + subordinate) ÷ Total C-units.**

An SI of 1.0 means every C-unit contains exactly one clause — no subordination at all. An SI of 1.5 means, on average, each C-unit contains 1.5 clauses — subordination is present roughly half the time. The higher the SI, the more syntactically complex the language.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Main vs. Subordinate Clause"]

To score SI, you identify clause types in each C-unit.

A **main clause** has a subject and a finite verb and can stand alone. Every C-unit has at least one.

A **subordinate clause** is dependent — introduced by a subordinating conjunction (because, when, that, while, unless, if), or functioning as a relative clause or noun clause.

[pause]

Examples:

*"He ran because he was late."* — Two clauses: "He ran" (main) + "because he was late" (subordinate). SI contribution: 2 clauses ÷ 1 C-unit.

*"I think that she went home."* — Two clauses: "I think" (main) + "that she went home" (noun clause). 2 ÷ 1.

*"She smiled."* — One clause. 1 ÷ 1.

[pause]

[screen: platform screenshot — WorkflowPanel Extra step → SI panel open]

In the platform, SI scoring lives in **WorkflowPanel Step 4 — Extra → Score SI.**

The SI panel shows you each C-unit from the transcript. For each one, you select a code:

- **[SI-1]** — one clause (main only)
- **[SI-2]** — two clauses
- **[SI-3]** — three or more clauses
- **[SI-0]** — omitted subject (counts but without the subject)
- **[SI-X]** — excluded (unintelligible, abandoned, or examiner error — doesn't count toward the composite)

[pause]

As you code, the **live composite score** at the top updates automatically: total clauses divided by total scoreable C-units. You can see the SI building in real time.

[pause]

The collapsible "Scoring rules" accordion in the panel gives you key rules from the SI guide — edge cases like dialogue, commands, and infinitive phrases — right there while you're coding. You don't have to leave the platform to look something up.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Interpreting SI Clinically"]

When the report generates, SI appears with the database comparison. Beyond ±1.5 SD means syntactic simplicity is clinically significant — even when MLU looks typical. That's the value of SI: it catches what sentence length alone misses.

[pause]

When SI is low, your intervention targets become specific: embedding relative clauses, using subordinating conjunctions to express cause and reason, practicing complex sentence expansion in structured tasks.

[pause]

Module 7 in review: SI measures clause density per C-unit. Use the platform's SI panel to code each C-unit and watch the composite calculate live. Low SI beyond ±1.5 SD is a flag for syntactic intervention, independent of MLU.

## MODULE 8: Scoring Narrative, Expository & Persuasive Samples

Sentence-level measures tell us how a child constructs individual utterances. But communication requires more than well-formed sentences — it requires **organization**. Can a child tell a coherent story? Explain something clearly? Build a logical argument?

That's what the SALT scoring schemes measure.

[screen: slide — "NSS — 7 Characteristics (max 35)"]

**The Narrative Scoring Scheme evaluates seven components of story structure,** each scored 0–5.

**Introduction** — does the child establish setting and characters at the opening?

**Character Development** — are characters described beyond just named? Actions, appearance, personality?

**Mental and Emotional States** — does the child reference characters' thoughts, feelings, motivations? This is tied to theory of mind and is often a sensitive marker of language disorder.

**Referencing and Listener Awareness** — does the child use pronouns accurately so the listener always knows who's being referred to?

**Conflict, Resolution, and Event/Reaction** — does the child identify the central problem and resolve it?

**Cohesion** — does the narrative hold together as a whole? Are ideas connected and sequenced logically?

**Conclusion** — does the story end — a closing statement, a moral, a return to the starting state?

[pause]

Research shows NSS scores are highly sensitive to language disorder and predictive of literacy outcomes. A child scoring 1–2 on multiple components is showing you exactly where narrative intervention needs to focus.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "ESS — 10 Characteristics (max 50)"]

**The Expository Scoring Scheme evaluates how well a child organizes informational language.** Ten characteristics — Object, Preparations, Start, Course of Play, Rules, Scoring, Duration, Strategy, Terminology, and Cohesion — map to the structure of an expository description task.

The ESS connects directly to academic language: how well a child explains something in an organized, complete, listener-oriented way. ESS scores that are low on Cohesion and Terminology often align with the academic language difficulties teachers describe.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "PSS — 7 Characteristics (max 35)"]

**The Persuasive Scoring Scheme evaluates argument structure.** Issue Identification, Supporting Reasons, Other Point of View, Compromises, Conclusion, Cohesion, and Effectiveness.

PSS is developmentally later — most children don't produce complete argument structures until middle school. It's particularly useful for adolescent caseloads and for students working on academic writing skills.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Connecting Scores to Intervention"]

Here's the clinical payoff of discourse-level scoring: it gives you **specific, actionable intervention targets.**

A child scoring 1 on Mental States in the NSS? Intervention targets perspective-taking language: "she felt scared because...", "he thought that..."

A child scoring 1 on Object and Terminology in the ESS? Work explicitly on expository language structure and domain vocabulary.

And because these scoring schemes are standardized and live in the platform, you can re-score a follow-up sample after intervention and show measurable discourse-level growth.

[pause]

## MODULE 9: Bilingual Assessment with SALT
**Target length:** 15 minutes (~1,950 words)

---

If your caseload includes children learning English as a second language — and depending on where you practice, that may describe a substantial portion of your clients — this module is essential.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "The Assessment Problem"]

Here's the challenge. When a bilingual child is referred for a language concern, you're facing a high-stakes diagnostic question: does this child have a **language disorder** — a true deficit in language processing — or do they have **limited English proficiency**, which is a completely normal outcome of learning a second language?

Getting this wrong has real consequences. Overdiagnosis places children in special education inappropriately. Underdiagnosis means a child with a genuine disorder goes without support.

[pause]

Standardized tests normed on monolingual English speakers cannot reliably answer this question. They weren't designed for bilingual populations and will consistently underestimate the language ability of L2 learners. This isn't a new finding — it's been documented in the research for decades. It's also not a subtle limitation. The bias is substantial.

[pause]

[screen: slide — Rojas & Iglesias (2009)]

In 2009, Raul Rojas and Aquiles Iglesias published "Making a Case for Language Sampling: Assessment and Intervention With Spanish/English Second-Language Learners" in The ASHA Leader — it won the Outstanding Contribution Award that year. Their core argument: language sample analysis with bilingual norms is an essential component of assessment for bilingual children, and it's more valid and more equitable than standardized testing alone.

[pause]

The key insight from their work: a bilingual child's language ability looks different depending on the task, the language, and the cognitive demand. A child may communicate fluently in casual conversation in both languages. In a demanding expository task in their weaker language, they may look significantly disordered. Basing a clinical decision on that weaker-language performance alone is not appropriate — and not defensible.

[pause]

The solution: **collect samples in both languages, using the same protocol, and compare each to bilingual norms.**

[pause]

[screen: platform screenshot — New Session wizard, Database step, with bilingual databases visible]

**The platform supports bilingual assessment directly.**

Create two sessions for the same speaker — one for the English sample, one for the Spanish sample. In the Database step, select the bilingual English or bilingual Spanish reference database. These databases contain samples from typically developing children who are also bilingual — not monolingual children. Their language profiles look different from monolinguals, and that difference is normal and expected.

[pause]

[screen: platform screenshot — two reports side by side: English vs. Spanish]

When you have both reports, you're looking for this pattern:

A child with a **language difference** (typical development, L2 learning) will show below-average measures in English — but typical or near-typical measures in Spanish compared to bilingual Spanish norms.

A child with a **language disorder** will show below-average measures in **both** languages, even when compared to appropriate bilingual norms. The deficit shows up across both language systems, not just in the weaker one.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Case Study: María, Age 7;3"]

Let me walk through the María case.

María is seven years old, in 1st grade, classified as an English Learner. Her clinician collected a narrative retell in English and the same retell in Spanish.

English report — compared to bilingual English norms: MLUw at -2.1 SD, NDW at -2.4 SD. Significantly below average.

Spanish report — compared to bilingual Spanish norms: MLUw at -0.6 SD, NDW at -0.4 SD. Within typical range.

[pause]

The clinical conclusion: María's English performance reflects language difference, not disorder. Her Spanish performance is typical for a bilingual child her age. If her clinician had assessed only in English — against monolingual norms — María might have received a disorder diagnosis she didn't warrant, with all the consequences that carries.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Elicitation in Two Languages"]

Eliciting samples in two languages uses the same protocol in both. Same picture book. Same instructions, translated into Spanish. Same examiner behavior — neutral, non-scaffolding, consistent timing. The goal is comparable samples: same task, different language.

The examiner needs to be proficient in both languages, or work with a bilingual SLP or a trained interpreter who understands the SALT protocol and what consistent elicitation requires.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Transcription Considerations for Bilingual Samples"]

When transcribing bilingual samples, a few things to know.

**Code-switching** — moving between languages within or across utterances — is a normal feature of bilingual communication. It's not an error. Transcribe the utterance as produced, noting the language of each portion if needed.

**Spanish morpheme conventions** follow the same principles as English — mark inflectional morphemes — but the specific morphemes differ. The SALT bilingual training materials include a Spanish morpheme reference guide.

**Dialectal variation.** Same rule as English — mark with [D] rather than as an error.

[pause]

Module 9 in review: language disorder and language difference are clinically distinct, and standardized English tests cannot distinguish them for bilingual learners. Collect samples in both languages. Use the platform's bilingual databases. Look for below-average performance across both languages as the marker of true disorder — not performance in L2 alone.

## MODULE 11: Outlier Codes & Edge Cases
**Target length:** 15 minutes (~1,950 words)

---

You've learned the core SALT conventions. This module is about the cases that break the rules — or look like they do. These are the situations where real transcribers make real errors, where the platform's validator will flag your transcript, or where you'll quietly produce incorrect measures without knowing it.

Every case in this module comes from the documented SALT outlier set. Work through these carefully — this is where accurate transcription separates from approximate transcription.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "The Morpheme Traps"]

Let's start with bound morphemes, because that's where most outlier errors cluster.

**/'s — three different meanings.**

The contraction /'s almost always means "is": `he/'s leaving.` Mark it.

But /z is possessive: `Sally/z house.` And /s is plural.

The notation looks similar but the meaning is different, and SALT tracks them separately. When you see 's in speech, ask: does this mean "is"? If yes — `/'s`. If it's possessive — `/z`. If it's plural possessive — `/s/z`. The order of /s/z doesn't matter to the engine.

[pause]

**The omitted morpheme.**

When a morpheme is missing — not just a word — use the asterisk after the slash: `boy/*z bed`. This tells SALT there was an omitted possessive morpheme. The asterisk here is not an omitted word — it's an omitted bound morpheme. Do not write `boy[EW:boy/z] bed`. Use `/*`.

[pause]

**Time and measurement possessives.**

When time or measurement is used in possessive form, mark /z: `a week/z time`, `the journey/z end`, `three day/s/z notice` (plural and possessive together). These are common in connected speech and easy to miss.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "The Contraction Set — Most of It Is Missing From Your Transcript"]

The full contraction set is much larger than most transcribers apply. Standard contractions that are always marked:

/'s (is), /'re (are), /'ll (will), /'m (am), /'d (would), /n't (not), /'t (not in informal forms), /'ve (have).

Non-standard contractions — also always marked:
/h's (has): `He/h's been sick.`
/d's (does): `What/d's he do?`
/d'd (did): `Why/d'd the boy look for the frog?`
/'us (us): `Let/'us go.`

[pause]

**Critical exceptions for /n't:**

Do NOT slash contractions where the root word sound changes. "Won't" is not "will not" in contracted form — the sound has changed. "Don't" similarly. Do not write `will/n't` or `do/n't` for won't or don't. And never mark ain't.

[pause]

**Let/'us, not let/'s.**

"Let's" means "let us" — the uncontracted form is "let us," not "let is." So: `let/'us go.` Not `let/'s go.`

[pause]

**Omitted contracted word.**

When a contracted word is omitted, you have two options. You can write `*I/'ll` — this tells SALT there's an omitted word AND an omitted bound morpheme, which is precise but assumes the speaker would have contracted. The more common approach is `*I *will` — omit both words separately. Either is acceptable; be consistent within a transcript.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Error Code Deep Dive"]

Let's revisit the error codes with precision.

**[EW] — Extraneous Word.** An extra word that makes the utterance ungrammatical and has no grammatical replacement. The test: could you swap a same-part-of-speech word in and make it grammatical? If yes — that's a substitution, not an extraneous word. If no — it's [EW].

Example: `She went for[EW] to the store.` There's no word you can replace "for" with to fix this utterance. It's just wrong. That's [EW].

Contrast: if a child says "She goed to the store," "goed" has an obvious replacement — "went." That's [EO], not [EW].

[pause]

**[EO] — Overgeneralization.** Format is always: `produced_form|root_word[EO:correct_form]`.

`C He falled|fall[EO:fell].`
`C There were deers|deer[EO:deer] in the woods.`
`C That/'s hises|his[EO:his].`
`C The next day the boy woked|wake[EO:woke] up.`

The pipe (|) separates what the child said from the root. The [EO] code contains the correct form.

**Do not slash a bound morpheme that is in error.** If a child says "bited," do not write `bite/ed`. Write `bited|bite[EO:bit]`. We don't credit a correctly formed morpheme on an incorrect form.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Gerund After a Preposition — Do Not /ING"]

This is one of the highest-frequency errors in SALT transcription. When an -ing word follows a preposition, it is a gerund — a noun, not a progressive verb. Do not mark /ING.

Examples:
- She insisted on **calling** him. → `calling` — no /ING
- I had gym before **going** to class. → `going` — no /ING
- Kept on **singing**. By **trying**. Without **falling**. For **winning**. About **going**. Before **getting**. After **eating**. → none get /ING

The tip: if you see an –ing word after a preposition, it's a gerund. No slash.

[pause]

**Gerund as complement.** When an -ing word follows a direct object as a complement — also no /ING.

`I found the guard sleeping.` → `sleeping` — no /ING.
`We consider the fish spoiled once it smell/3s like fish.` → `spoiled` — no /ING.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Emphasis Repetitions — the Pipe Operator"]

When a speaker repeats a word for emphasis — not as a maze — don't wrap the whole thing in maze parentheses. Instead, use the pipe operator to redefine the repetition.

`He laugh/ed and laughed_laughed_laughed|laugh/ed`

SALT analyzes what comes after the pipe, so the speaker gets credit for `laugh/ed` without the long bound-morpheme analysis error that a triple underscore would cause.

[pause]

**Proper names and bound morphemes.**

Do not mark bound morphemes on proper names or titles.

`Mother/z_Day` is wrong in two ways — bound morpheme on a proper name, and improper underscore. Correct: `Mothers_Day`.

`Children/z_Museum` — if it's a proper name (the specific museum), write `Children's_Museum`. If it's just any children's museum, `children/z museum` is fine.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Like as a Filled Pause — [FP]"]

"Like" as a filler is not just a maze word — it gets a specific code: `[FP]` (filled pause), placed inside the maze parentheses.

`He (like[FP]) run/3s so fast.`
`He (um like[FP] run/3s) run/3s so fast.` — here "like" is part of a larger maze that also contains a revision.

**Exceptions — do not code as [FP]:**

Simile: `She is like her dad.` — this is grammatical "like," not a filler.
Estimate or example: `He has like six dogs.` — "like" here means "approximately." No [FP].

The test: could you remove "like" and still have a grammatical, complete utterance? If yes — it's probably a filler, code it [FP]. If removing it changes the meaning — it's not a filler.

[pause]

[screen: slide — "Quick Reference — The Outlier Checklist"]

Before you submit any transcript, run through this list for the edge cases that catch people most often:

One: Did you mark /'s correctly — is it "is" (/'s), possessive (/z), plural-possessive (/s/z)?

Two: Did you catch time and measurement possessives — week/z time, journey/z end?

Three: Did you use the full contraction set — not just /n't?

Four: Did you avoid slashing won't, don't, ain't?

Five: Did you write let/'us, not let/'s?

Six: Did you use the [EO] format correctly — `produced|root[EO:correct]`?

Seven: Did you avoid slashing morphemes on error forms?

Eight: Did you skip /ING after prepositions and on gerund complements?

Nine: Did you use the pipe operator for emphasis repetitions instead of mazes?

Ten: Did you avoid slashing proper names?

Eleven: Did you code "like" as `(like[FP])` when it's a filler, and leave it uncoded when it's a simile or estimate?

[pause]

These aren't obscure edge cases. They appear in most samples from school-age speakers — which is exactly the population you're most likely to be assessing. Getting these right is what separates a transcript that produces valid measures from one that looks right but contains quiet errors.
